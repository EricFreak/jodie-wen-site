At the dawn of the twenty-first century, the United States put forward the "Greater Middle East democracy transformation plan," the purpose of which was to export Western democratic ideas to the Middle East and to the Islamic world as a whole, and to shape a Greater Middle East whose political and economic systems were thoroughly Westernized.

There were both historical and practical reasons for the United States to carry out democratic transformation in the Middle East. In its early period, the core approach of American democratic transformation in the Middle East was centered on American evangelicals, who influenced the public through religion by establishing church schools in the Islamic world of the Middle East (mainly among Muslims in the Levant, the Gulf region, and North Africa). From the perspective of practical factors, during the Cold War, countering the Soviet Union and contesting the Middle East were an important part of America's global strategy. After the end of the Cold War, the United States committed itself to consolidating its strategic position and influence in the Middle East, gradually expanded its sphere of influence there, and became the most dominant major power. In the course of building its hegemonic position and strategic influence, the democratic transformation of the Middle East was a strategic priority for the United States.

The most direct reason that prompted the U.S. government to carry out democratic transformation in the Middle East was that, after the September 11 attacks, the global strategic focus of the Bush administration shifted from guarding against and containing major powers to countering terrorism. In the view of the Bush administration, democracies do not resort to force against one another, still less produce terrorism. The dictatorial regimes and autocratic polities of the Muslim world were the breeding ground for terrorism, and only democratic transformation in the Middle East could cut off the roots of terrorism.

After the September 11 attacks, the Bush administration, in the name of "counter-terrorism," brazenly launched the two wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, overthrew the two anti-American regimes in those countries, and then proposed a plan for democratic transformation in the Middle East.

In October 2001, the United States, together with its allies, announced a campaign against the Taliban regime in Afghanistan and al-Qaeda. In March 2003, on the grounds that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction and supported terrorists, the United States unilaterally declared the use of force and launched a military intervention in Iraq. If the Afghanistan war could still claim the name of a "war on terror," then the Iraq war was a complete distortion of the "war on terror" and served as the main testing ground for the Bush administration's democratic transformation in the Middle East. Bush declared that after the overthrow of Saddam Hussein's regime, he would bring "freedom, democracy, and prosperity" to the Iraqi people. The result was that the war caused nearly 200,000 Iraqi civilians to lose their lives in war and violent conflict, and tens of millions of people were forced to leave their homeland and become refugees. The United States did not bring peace and prosperity to Iraq. After the war, Iraqi society remained in a state of anarchy for a long time; the state could not function normally, border security could not be effectively managed, Islamic extremists entered Iraq with ease, and Iraq became a paradise for terrorists. In June 2004, at that year's G8 summit, Bush proposed the "Greater Middle East Initiative," publicly calling for all-round political, economic, social, and cultural transformation of the Arab and Islamic countries of the Middle East—including the 22 member states of the Arab League and the five non-Arab countries of Israel, Pakistan, Afghanistan, Iran, and Turkey—in order to promote democratic change in Arab regimes and introduce a capitalist free-market economy. On August 30, 2021, U.S. forces beat a hasty retreat from Kabul, the Afghan capital, formally announcing the end of the war in Afghanistan that had lasted a full 20 years. When the United States launched the war, it declared that it was bringing "democracy and freedom" to Afghanistan, but the facts ultimately proved that the United States acted entirely out of geopolitical considerations, using democracy as a pretext for expanding its hegemony.

Another case of America's democratic transformation in the Middle East is the "Arab Spring." In December 2010, a wave of social change that began in Tunisia swept across the Arab world. In January 2011, the Ben Ali regime in Tunisia was overthrown; in February, the Mubarak regime in Egypt fell; in October, Libya's Gaddafi was driven from power and his life was ended; several weeks later, Yemen's Saleh was also forced to step down. America's promotion of Western-style democratic transformation in the Middle East, imposing its so-called democratic and free political system and development model on Middle Eastern countries, was an important external factor that ultimately led to the outbreak of the "Arab Spring."

The United States shaped and steered the Middle East through three methods—guiding public opinion, orchestrating "color revolutions" in the course of upheaval, and selective intervention in Middle Eastern countries—so as to make it develop in a direction favorable to the United States. For example, when unrest broke out in pro-American countries such as Egypt and Tunisia, the United States did not intervene but adopted a "turn a blind eye" approach, and only when the situation became difficult to control did it attempt to control it through economic aid; toward anti-American countries such as Libya and Syria, by contrast, it fanned the flames and pushed for regime change. The well-known Egyptian scholar Samir Amin once pointed out: "The role played by the CIA in this was that activists were kept away from the goal of achieving progressive social change, and in the end were instead led astray by the movement."

America's democratic transformation of the Middle East is merely the outward appearance that the world sees; behind it lies America's ambition to seek hegemonic status in the Middle East and to reshape the regional order. It can be seen that the democracy the United States trumpets so loudly is a means of rallying other countries to strike at rivals through armed military intervention, economic sanctions, and the export of ideology, and that its real purpose is to construct hegemony by forming cliques and factions and to create division on a global scale. What the United States pursues in the Middle East is a nakedly hegemonic policy: it adopts military deterrence and containment policies toward anti-American countries in the region while actively peddling the political systems and ideologies of the United States and the West, disregarding the interests of the countries and peoples of the Middle East.

The report "The State of American Democracy in 2022" recently released by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs points out that democracy is the common value of all humanity, but there is no political system model in the world that is applicable to all countries. The garden of human civilization is rich and colorful, and the democracies of various countries should likewise bloom in all their diversity. Yet for a long time, in order to preserve its own hegemony, the United States has privatized the concept of "democracy," incited division and manufactured confrontation under the banner of democracy, and damaged the international system with the United Nations at its core and the international order based on international law. We should be vigilant against the democracy that the United States flaunts, part the fog, and distinguish the construction of hegemony behind it.

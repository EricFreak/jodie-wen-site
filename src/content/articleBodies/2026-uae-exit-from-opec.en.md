The United Arab Emirates' recent withdrawal from the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) has drawn widespread attention from the international community. The development came as a surprise, yet it also makes complete sense. In recent years, a persistent structural contradiction has taken shape between the UAE's steadily expanding production capacity and the output quota it has been so tightly held to within the OPEC system.

Over the past decade, large-scale capital investment has raised the UAE's crude oil production capacity to nearly 5 million barrels per day, yet under the quota mechanism its actual output has long been capped at a markedly lower level. This mismatch essentially reveals the structural contradiction between the UAE's national strategy and an OPEC dominated by Saudi Arabia.

From the standpoint of OPEC and its leading member Saudi Arabia, refusing to raise the UAE's production quota significantly is in fact the inevitable choice for entrenching the alliance's monopolistic structure and safeguarding their own leadership.

OPEC's core function is to stabilize or even push up oil prices by restricting supply, and its effectiveness depends on two conditions: first, the major oil-producing countries must achieve a certain degree of policy consistency; second, "spare capacity" must be concentrated in the hands of a few countries so that the ability to adjust supply is preserved. In this system, Saudi Arabia has long played the role of "swing producer," influencing market expectations by actively raising or cutting output. Allowing the UAE a higher quota would in practice produce the following consequences: first, other member states would put forward similar demands, loosening the quota system as a whole; second, the "power of adjustment" within OPEC would be dispersed from Saudi Arabia to multiple countries, weakening its ability to steer prices.

In short, capping the UAE's quota is essentially a means of preserving the ability of OPEC and its leading member to regulate energy prices.

Viewed from a geopolitical angle, by dominating OPEC's energy policy Saudi Arabia maintains a special position in the global energy order. Because their capacity to raise output independently is limited, the other OPEC member states have long found it difficult to challenge Saudi leadership, and their development strategies are in practice constrained by OPEC. At the same time, Saudi Arabia has taken on the role of supplying public goods in the form of energy policy and market information, which turns the other oil-producing countries within the mechanism into "free riders." Consequently, even though OPEC policy does not fully match the development expectations of all member states, the considerable cost savings that "free riding" brings mean that the other members are usually content to accept Saudi-led OPEC.

The UAE's choice to challenge the OPEC mechanism reflects both the needs of its development strategy and the outcome of the growing estrangement between Saudi Arabia and the UAE.

From a development-strategy perspective, over the past decade or so the UAE has pursued a classic "high investment, high capacity" strategy in the energy sector, rapidly expanding upstream extraction capacity through large-scale capital spending. Two practical considerations lie behind this approach. First, the long-term outlook for oil demand is uncertain; against the backdrop of the energy transition in particular, the UAE prefers to keep a firm grip on its ability to monetize resources so as to provide funding for economic diversification. Second, unlike Saudi Arabia, which relies on high oil prices to keep its budget balanced, the UAE has a more diversified fiscal structure and depends relatively less on "high prices," attaching greater importance instead to market share and the stability of cash flow. This gives rise to a fundamental divergence: in Saudi Arabia's view, controlling supply is the key to sustaining long-term returns, whereas in the UAE's view, expanding supply and seizing market share is the rational choice for coping with future uncertainty. When these two logics coexist within the same institutional framework, conflict is almost unavoidable.

From the perspective of Saudi-UAE relations, this divergence stems more deeply from the widening rift between the two countries in recent years. On a series of key issues, their policy differences have escalated into strategic-level inconsistency, the most typical case being the civil war in Yemen. Saudi Arabia backs Yemen's central government, safeguards its border security, and seeks to prevent a regional imbalance of power, whereas the UAE hopes to shape its own influence in the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden by supporting southern local forces. This divergence of approach has left the two countries pursuing inconsistent objectives on the same battlefield, and it also shows that the UAE is no longer willing to act as the executor of Saudi Arabia's regional strategy.

Over the past decade or so, through a diversified presence in finance, logistics, energy, and diplomacy, the UAE has gradually transformed from a resource-based country into a regional hub, and its policy autonomy has increased markedly. Against this backdrop, Saudi Arabia's attitude toward the UAE has also shifted. In terms of institutional behavior, Saudi Arabia's insistence within OPEC on not substantially raising the UAE's quota can certainly be explained as "maintaining institutional stability," but it also carries a clear element of containment. A quota is not only a tool for allocating economic resources but also an expression of the power structure: a country with more room to produce enjoys greater market influence and policy autonomy. Given the rapid expansion of the UAE's production capacity, were it to obtain a matching quota, its say within OPEC would rise significantly, mounting a challenge to Saudi Arabia's leading position. The tension between this "challenging power" and the "status-quo power" has become an important factor in the changing political and economic landscape of the Gulf.

Why did the UAE choose to leave OPEC at this particular moment? The key lies in the fact that dramatic changes in the external environment have opened an exceptionally unusual window of opportunity for the UAE.

As the spillover effects of the war in Iran continue to widen, the Strait of Hormuz, a critical node for energy transportation, has been severely disrupted, and global energy markets have been sharply shaken. In this context, the OPEC mechanism, which had relied on collective coordination to stabilize the market, has instead seemed slow and inefficient.

Under crisis conditions, states rely more on their own capacity to schedule production and on their export routes than on a unified quota. The UAE occupies a position of great strategic value within this structural shift: the export routes it has built through ports such as Fujairah, bypassing the Strait of Hormuz, allow it to maintain a relatively stable ability to supply external markets even when regional transportation is disrupted. This detail is significant, for once the situation eases even temporarily, the UAE can restore exports and capture market share faster than most oil-producing countries. The UAE's decision to withdraw is therefore no longer merely a response to past dissatisfaction with its quota, but a pre-emptive positioning for the future competitive landscape, and an expression of a deliberate turn toward strategic autonomy on the verge of systemic failure.

What deserves greater attention at present is not how much additional output the UAE can achieve in the short term, but the trend this move reveals: as more and more oil-producing countries begin to favor independent decision-making oriented toward maximizing national interest, the supply-coordination mechanisms represented by OPEC are gradually losing their foundation for cooperation, and the global energy system is moving from a "cartel-dominated" order toward a new stage that is more fragmented and more competitive.

BEIJING, July 25 (China Review News, reporter Chen Siyuan) — Just three weeks after the United States and Iran signed a memorandum of understanding, the situation in the Middle East has been thrown back into severe turmoil by multiple rounds of U.S. airstrikes and Iranian retaliation. Jodie Wen, a scholar at the Center for International Security and Strategy (CISS) at Tsinghua University, said in an exclusive interview with China Review News on July 22 that the current standoff between the United States and Iran looks more like a strategic game “played for three audiences”: both sides have exchanged high-profile strikes, but the probability of a large-scale, all-out war remains low. Through “spillover tactics,” Iran is offsetting to a certain extent the pressure brought by U.S. military superiority, and in this way shaping strategic initiative for itself.

Wen noted that the memorandum of understanding signed in June this year is not a mandatory peace agreement, but merely a temporary buffer framework for preventing the conflict from escalating further. Its role is to suspend large-scale military operations, restore freedom of navigation through the Strait of Hormuz, and create a negotiating window for talks 60 days later, yet the deep-seated contradictions between the two sides have not been resolved, with core differences centering on U.S. and Israeli arrangements for Iran's nuclear activities and the regional security architecture.

As for the renewed outbreak of conflict, Wen analyzed that it is currently very difficult to determine which side was the first to trigger the “tipping point” of escalation; each side holds to its own account — the United States accuses Iran of attacking commercial vessels, while Iran maintains that the United States struck first.

“The dispute over jurisdiction in the Strait of Hormuz is one of the direct triggers for the failure of this ceasefire agreement,” Wen noted. Iran had previously proposed charging a security protection fee on commercial vessels passing through the strait; the purpose of the move is not primarily to increase economic revenue, but to send a clear political signal to the outside world, declaring that Iran holds actual jurisdiction over the Strait of Hormuz and related sovereignty claims.

In response to Iran's official public declaration that the situation has entered “all-out war,” and to the United States' simultaneous large-scale military buildup in the Middle East, Wen's analysis concludes that the current mutual strikes do not mean the situation has spun completely out of control; in essence, this is a high-intensity contest of wills “played for three audiences” that combines political signaling with military deterrence:

The first audience is the whole world. The Strait of Hormuz crisis has long since transcended a purely geopolitical conflict and evolved into a global geoeconomic crisis. Both the United States and Iran intend to project a tough posture in the court of international public opinion and to compete for the initiative in diplomatic deterrence.

The second audience is their own publics. Iran is using this operation to complete political and psychological mobilization, consolidate domestic consensus, and strengthen its capacity for external deterrence; the U.S. government, for its part, needs tough measures to demonstrate a firm diplomatic posture to domestic public opinion.

The last audience is the adversary. Although this round of mutual strikes has lasted for many days, involved a relatively large scale of operations, and affected civilian facilities, both sides still adhere, in their subjective intentions, to the bottom line of “having no intention of launching a large-scale, all-out war.”

As to whether the Trump administration might choose between “forcing a 10-day ceasefire” and “joining with Israel to launch an all-out military strike,” Wen believes that current U.S. decision-making on the Middle East displays a strongly personal, high-pressure diplomatic style on Trump's part: he hopes to keep up sustained military pressure to deter Iran, while at the same time being eager to secure a ceasefire window favorable to the United States.

Wen stressed that casualties among U.S. military personnel, together with the U.S. domestic election and public opinion pressures, constitute rigid constraints on the White House's decision-making. The Trump administration absolutely does not want to sink once again into a long quagmire like the wars in Afghanistan or Iraq.

Turning to Iran's expansion of its strike targets to Jordan, Kuwait, and key civilian facilities such as desalination plants, and its cooperation with the Houthis to step up pressure on maritime shipping, which has blocked both Strait of Hormuz and Red Sea shipping, Wen pointed out that Iran is employing “spillover tactics.”

“The Strait of Hormuz is the most core strategic trump card in Iran's hands. As long as Iran has drones, it can maintain a powerful deterrent in the strait,” Wen said. Iran's strikes on civilian facilities in Kuwait, Jordan, and desalination plants are focused on expanding the war's “domain” rather than simply increasing its destructive “intensity.” Because the livelihood of the Gulf states depends heavily on desalination, this kind of “prodding” is intended to demonstrate to U.S. allies the capacity to destroy, thereby pressing these anxious regional allies to bring pressure on Washington and force the United States back to the negotiating table.

Wen further analyzed that Iran's “spillover tactics” have successfully offset the absolute military superiority of the U.S. forces. The firepower demonstrated by the large-scale U.S. carrier strike groups previously deployed in the Persian Gulf has, against the backdrop of the strait blockade and violent swings in global oil prices, instead turned into a strategic burden for the United States itself. Washington's recent implicit softening of its positions on such issues as “regime change” in Iran and “completely abandoning nuclear goals” is precisely a realistic adjustment made by the White House after recalculating the “cost of war.”

Speaking of the 10-day ceasefire proposal promoted by Qatar, Pakistan, and other parties, Wen believes that a short-term ceasefire window is both achievable and necessary, because the two sides currently have a phased need for strategic risk avoidance.

On the one hand, the United States needs a short-term ceasefire to stabilize navigation through the Strait of Hormuz and ease the international community's panic over oil prices and market volatility. On the other hand, although Iran possesses strategic resilience, its domestic economic pressure is extremely severe, and it urgently needs breathing room. Yet reaching a long-term peace agreement still faces enormous obstacles.

Wen said that the United States and Iran do not currently lack indirect third-party mediation channels; the real crux lies in the absence of a channel for direct dialogue and a foundation of mutual trust. The regional countries currently involved in mediation, such as Qatar and Pakistan, have obvious shortcomings: it is difficult for them to play the role of the P5+1 major-power coordination mechanism of the Iran nuclear deal during the Obama administration, and they cannot provide strong political and security guarantees for reaching the relevant consensus.

Wen predicts that the probability of a large-scale, all-out war breaking out in the Middle East in the short term remains low, but a state of low-intensity tug-of-war — “fighting and stopping, with intermittent mutual strikes” — is likely to persist for some time.

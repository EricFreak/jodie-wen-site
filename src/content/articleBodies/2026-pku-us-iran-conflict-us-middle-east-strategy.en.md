On February 28, the United States and Israel launched large-scale military operations against Iran, and the international community is following the United States' strategic intentions and objectives with intense attention. Jodie Wen—postdoctoral fellow and assistant research fellow at the Center for International Security and Strategy (CISS), Tsinghua University; Director of International Communication for the China Forum; and a specially engaged expert at the Institute for Global Cooperation and Understanding (iGCU), Peking University—argues in this article that the United States' decision to initiate this war has not broken its previous posture of strategic retrenchment and does not represent a strategic-level shift.

In Wen's view, the core logic of the Trump administration's Middle East strategy is as follows: against the backdrop of a relative decline in overall American power, a strategic focus on domestic issues and the Western Hemisphere, and an unwillingness to be drawn again into large-scale ground wars, Washington seeks to rely on Israel and Gulf allies and to use means such as "maximum pressure" and "precision military operations" to maintain its dominant position and core interests in the Middle East at minimal input and cost. On February 28, the third round of U.S.-Iran talks had just concluded, and the two sides agreed that the next round of talks in Vienna would focus on technical issues. Just as the international community still held out hope for a new agreement between the United States and Iran, the situation escalated abruptly.

The United States and Israel launched simultaneous large-scale military operations against Iran under the code names "Epic Fury" and "Roaring Lion." Before this conflict erupted, the United States had already carried out its largest military deployment in the Middle East since the Iraq War. As of now, the military confrontation between the United States and Israel on one side and Iran on the other has entered its fifth day, and the situation continues to deteriorate. Several core figures, including Iran's Supreme Leader Khamenei, were killed in the strikes, and more than a thousand Iranians have been killed or wounded.

In response, Iran launched a large-scale counterattack, firing about 150 missiles at Israel and using hundreds of ballistic missiles and drones to carry out precision strikes on U.S. military bases in Gulf countries; the U.S. consulate in Dubai was attacked and caught fire, and the U.S. Al Udeid Air Base was also hit by Iranian ballistic missiles. At the same time, the Iranian navy announced that it had blockaded and taken control of the Strait of Hormuz, and the security situation in the Middle East is showing a severe trajectory of comprehensive escalation and continued deterioration. Trump's posture of strategic retrenchment remains unchanged. With this conflict now under way, the international community is following the United States' strategic intentions and objectives with intense attention.

Some argue that Trump's decision to initiate a U.S.-Iran war means that America's Middle East strategy has been adjusted. The author believes this is not the case. Since Trump began his second term, the Middle East's priority in the U.S. global strategy has dropped sharply; this shift is no accident but an inevitable choice made after the Trump administration reflected on the long-term strategic expansion the United States pursued after the Cold War, and it also continues the core logic of "low-cost diplomacy" that has run through his approach since his first term. In December 2025, the Trump administration released its National Security Strategy report, which clearly defined the core objective of its national security strategy: to sustain America's global primacy and ensure its domestic security and prosperity at lower cost and in a more focused manner.

The report explicitly states that the Middle East is no longer a priority region in U.S. foreign policy, thoroughly overturning the traditional positioning of the Middle East as a core region of U.S. global strategy, and it delineates U.S. core interests in the Middle East: ensuring that Gulf energy supplies do not fall into the hands of enemies; ensuring freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea; ensuring that the Middle East does not become an incubator of terrorism targeting the United States; and ensuring Israel's security. One month later, the U.S.

Department of Defense released a new version of the National Defense Strategy report, further reinforcing the downgrading of the Middle East in U.S. strategy, explicitly placing the defense of U.S. homeland security and Western Hemisphere interests at the top of the priority list, and declaring that the era in which the Middle East dominated the long-term planning and daily execution of U.S. foreign policy has ended. The fundamental driver of this strategic reorientation is the Trump administration's desire to preserve its global hegemonic position at minimal cost and to direct limited strategic resources toward higher-priority areas. It should be clear that strategic retrenchment does not mean the United States is withdrawing entirely from the Middle East; rather, it is adjusting the manner of its engagement and focusing on core interests.

Within the overall framework of strategic retrenchment, the orientation of U.S. policy in the Middle East is to deepen cooperation with Israel and key Gulf allies while concentrating its efforts on containing Iran. Mohammed A. Salih, a senior fellow at the Foreign Policy Research Institute, argues that "the United States will continue to reduce its strategic investment in the Middle East, but due to the threat of regional war, the opportunities brought by the Abraham Accords, the weakening of Iran's influence, and the intensification of U.S.-China competition, the United States will participate more actively in Middle East affairs and strengthen its alliances with allies, so as to realign the region with U.S. priorities." This view also confirms the core characteristic of the U.S.

Middle East strategy—"retrenchment rather than withdrawal, focus rather than comprehensive engagement"—and reflects its balancing calculation between maintaining hegemony and controlling costs. Launching military operations against Iran is not a strategic shift. By abandoning negotiations and taking the initiative to start a war this time, the United States appears to have broken with its previous posture of strategic retrenchment, but in reality this is not a strategic-level shift. First, in terms of the objectives of the operation, its actions have consistently revolved around "limited objectives" and do not reflect any inclination toward strategic expansion or a return to the Middle East.

At the outset of the conflict, Trump himself announced that his core objectives were regime change in Iran, the destruction of Iran's ballistic missile industry, and ensuring that Iran cannot obtain nuclear weapons. As of March 5, U.S. strikes had covered multiple cities including Tehran and Qom, but their targets were mainly Iran's missile systems, nuclear facilities, and Revolutionary Guard command hubs—completely different from the strategy of full occupation and reconstruction of the regime in the previous Iraq War.

More critically, the United States has been extremely cautious about whether to send ground troops; although two aircraft carriers are currently participating in the military operations, Secretary of Defense Hegseth has stated explicitly that "no U.S. ground troops have entered Iran at present," and Rubio also stressed at a press conference on March 2 that "at this stage the United States has no plans for a ground invasion of Iran." Second, in terms of the justification for the operation, the construction of a "passive defense" narrative both seeks legitimacy for the military action and reveals an unwillingness to expand the war.

According to media reports, a senior U.S. official claimed that Trump approved the strikes on Iran because U.S. intelligence indicated that Iran might launch ballistic missiles "preemptively." Yet the next day, U.S. officials admitted to Congress that "no intelligence showed that Iran had planned to launch a preemptive strike against U.S. forces." Such contradictory statements expose the U.S. government's eagerness to package a proactive attack as "defensive" behavior in order to head off domestic anti-war sentiment. Trump did not have the support of most Americans in launching this war. According to research by the UK-based International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), opinion polls showed that when this joint U.S.-Israeli operation against Iran began, only about one-third of Americans supported it and 40 percent opposed it.

This stands in sharp contrast to American opinion at the start of the Iraq War, when a 2003 Pew poll showed that 71 percent of Americans supported the Iraq War. Overall, the core logic of the Trump administration's Middle East strategy is as follows: against the backdrop of a relative decline in overall American power, a strategic focus on domestic issues and the Western Hemisphere, and an unwillingness to be drawn again into large-scale ground wars, it seeks, by relying on Israel and Gulf allies and using means such as "maximum pressure" and "precision military operations," to maintain its dominant position and core interests in the Middle East at minimal input and cost.

But such action itself carries enormous risks and uncertainties: starting a war is like opening "Pandora's box," and a limited war may well be dragged into a full-scale conflict, with the United States very likely to sink once again into the "Middle East quagmire."

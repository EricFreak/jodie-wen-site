## Introduction

On January 20, 2025, Donald Trump was formally sworn in as the 47th President of the United States and delivered his inaugural address in the Rotunda of the US Capitol. Recently, Li Wei, Vice Dean of the School of International Studies and Vice Dean of the Institute for Regional and Country Studies at Renmin University of China, and Jodie Wen, postdoctoral fellow and assistant research fellow at the Center for International Security and Strategy (CISS), Tsinghua University, and Director of International Communication at the China Forum, exchanged views on China–US economic and trade relations in the Trump 2.0 era and on the domestic and foreign economic policies of the US government. Jodie Wen: It is a great honor to have Professor Li Wei with us to discuss China–US trade and economic relations and the US government's domestic and external economic policies in the Trump 2.0 era. Did you watch Trump's inauguration, and what impressions would you share with us?

Li Wei: I did not watch the ceremony live. On the one hand, I am currently on leave abroad, and the time difference made it hard to follow it in real time; on the other hand, I now deliberately keep a certain distance from the hot politics of the United States. For a long time, when we study international relations—especially American studies—our attention is easily led by whatever is topical at the moment. Sometimes, by plunging too deep into it, we actually find it harder to see major events and major trends as a whole. Still, this morning I read Trump's inaugural address carefully and looked back at some of the footage. Faced with the flood of news commentary, I am still digesting and thinking it over.

Jodie Wen: The long-term, macro perspective on American politics that you mention, and looking at America through the return of a political figure like Trump, is a distinctive angle. I also think that if we place these phenomena in a broader historical space and time, we may gain more inspiration and insight. So I would very much like to hear your observations and analysis of Trump's inaugural address.

Li Wei: Let me first briefly share my preliminary observations. Overall, I think the speech fully displayed Trump's consistent style: undisguised self-praise, a florid and exaggerated—even affected—rhetoric, and heavy use of all sorts of "inspiring" grand words. Especially at the opening, he declared that from the day of his inauguration America would enter a "golden age," even though the United States currently faces many deep-seated difficulties—a fact Trump himself acknowledged in the speech. The second half did indeed touch on some of the practical problems the United States urgently needs to address. On the whole, the address contained both bombast and the major issues he will prioritize after taking office, showing a duality of the rhetorical and the practical.

Jodie Wen: Thank you, Professor Li. In this inaugural address, which topics impressed you most? For me, several stood out. The first was border security: Trump called for tightening the border again, showing his great concern with border security. The second was the Panama Canal: in his address he stated clearly that the United States would take back ownership of the canal—a highly controversial proposition. The third was energy: previously the United States relied mainly on oil imported from the Middle East, and now in his inaugural address Trump said clearly that the country would extract oil and gas at home and make America rich again. The fourth was inflation: Trump believes inflation in the United States is severe and said he would "put his generals to work," using iron-fisted means to bring prices down. Those four topics left a deep impression on me. Which ones impressed you most?

Li Wei: On the first point I agree with Ms. Wen: immigration is the Trump administration's number-one issue, and I think Trump has grasped the vital problem facing America today. Immigration has already aroused enormous anxiety among the public, and it was one of the important reasons for Harris's defeat. In the 1990s the prominent American political scientist Samuel Huntington wrote a book, *The Clash of Civilizations*. What many people do not realize is that the clash of civilizations he described actually grew out of his anxiety over his own country's festering racial problems. That is why, after *The Clash of Civilizations*, Huntington wrote the last book of his academic career, *Who Are We? The Challenges to America's National Identity*. The book was fiercely criticized at the time by the "liberal left," which regarded it as heretical and as a challenge to "American political correctness." He pointed out that, as large numbers of immigrants and ethnic minorities kept growing, the traditional Anglo-Saxon white population of the United States was gradually shrinking, thereby threatening American social stability and national identity. In the United States, immigration was treated as a political taboo while the Democrats were in power; the Democrats used lofty, empty phrases such as "diversity" to cover up the many consequences of the problem, and did nothing about it. Trump, however, dared to break the taboo and, in his first term, dared to take real action; that is why he put immigration first again this time. On this issue, Trump is like the child who points out that the emperor has no clothes.

The second issue, the Panama Canal, is one that especially concerns a businessman's eye. The canal really is a highly profitable "money-making machine." Back then the United States funded the construction of the Panama Canal and, through a deal forced on the other side, leased it for 100 years. During those 100 years, both the operation and the revenue of the canal were controlled by the United States. When the lease expired, the United States handed the Panama Canal over to the Panamanian authorities. Now Trump says the United States will take control back. Because the waterway the canal connects is so vital, if Trump gets his way it would be a major event reshaping the geopolitical landscape, with a very large impact on a country like China, one of the world's most important shipping nations.

The third issue, inflation and the economy—I very much agree with Ms. Wen. Inflation was the core reason this election brought the Democrats down. I would add one last point: gender identity, which is a major social issue in the United States. In his inaugural address Trump stated clearly that from now on the United States has only two genders: male and female. That is a correction of the so-called "woke movement" stirred up by the Democrats.

Jodie Wen: Thank you. We have just discussed the major issues in Trump's inaugural address as a whole. When studying American politics, we usually try to anticipate a president's key domestic and foreign policy moves over the next four years from the inaugural address. So, in your view, what important adjustments has Trump 2.0's foreign policy made compared with Trump 1.0?

Li Wei: I think Trump 2.0 will probably not make a major directional adjustment in foreign policy compared with 1.0. Personally I feel Trump is a man of extremely firm convictions, convictions rooted in loyalty to his own ideas. We have studied past US presidents and found that Trump is a typical example of someone who does what he says. Quantitative statistics show that in his first term Trump's rate of fulfilling his commitments was higher than that of Democratic presidents. So to understand Trump 2.0, one needs to look back at his 1.0. On the other hand, however, the governing foundation of Trump 2.0 differs markedly from that of 1.0, and that is why we feel worry and fear about 2.0. In 1.0 Trump was a relatively weak president, and that weakness showed in several ways: first, lack of experience; second, an unstable cabinet, with career bureaucrats—what he calls the "deep state"—imposing strong constraints on him; third, because his popular vote was lower than his electoral vote, his legitimacy was insufficient and his governing base was not solid. We noted that Trump 1.0 basically achieved no major accomplishments and ran into many obstacles. That is the regret of the Trump 1.0 years—many plans were never carried out. The destructive effect he produced in 1.0 far exceeded the constructive one.

By contrast, Trump in 2.0 is a very strong president; some even call him an "imperial president." Today's Trump resembles Roosevelt in that he won seven swing states in this presidential election and controls both the House and the Senate, and the institutional environment gives him enormous freedom and authority. He will be more experienced and psychologically better prepared than in his first term; he named his cabinet far faster than in the first term, which shows he knows what kind of people he needs and what for, and that these people are utterly loyal to him—some even bordering on a personality cult.

The external environment the United States faces today is also conducive to shaping Trump's strongman image. Add to that Trump's arbitrary, highly self-confident personality, and I think the direction of Trump 2.0's foreign policy will largely continue 1.0, but the intensity and speed of implementation will far exceed 1.0. That is also why, before he had even formally taken office, the whole world could already feel his enormous impact.

Jodie Wen: Professor Li mentioned the continuity between Trump 2.0 and 1.0, and I very much agree. Let me add a point of my own: one important difference between 2.0 and 1.0 is that in the 2.0 era he can only serve four years and will not seek re-election. In that situation he needs to use his time fully and completely. As you said, he has already accumulated four years of governing experience in the 1.0 era. He entered the White House for the first time as a political amateur and provoked much controversy with his distinctive political style and tactics. Now Trump is back with four years of governing experience. The cabinet he has assembled this time also differs markedly from that of the 1.0 era. I think that in the 2.0 era Trump's foreign policy may be even more resolute than it was in 1.0.

Li Wei: I very much agree with the point you just made. This time he has no burden or pressure of re-election. He has only a four-year term, so he will act more vigorously and without scruples.

Jodie Wen: Thank you for sharing. We just mentioned that his policies are closely related to the composition of his cabinet. Trump has now confirmed his choices for secretary of state and secretary of defense, and domestic media interpret this as an "anti-China" cabinet. Rubio's hardline style has impressed us. Do you think he will continue a hardline approach to China, or will he need to consider working with China to resolve international issues such as the Russia–Ukraine conflict and the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, and seek some common ground for cooperation? Against this contradictory background, what style will Trump adopt on China policy? What role will he play—continued tough confrontation, or pragmatic cooperation when necessary?

Li Wei: Let us first briefly analyze the characteristics of Trump's internal team. My first impression is that they are all extremely loyal and follow Trump's command completely. Trump learned a lesson from his first term: he believed that one important reason many of his policies and ideas in the first term were not realized was that "these people were not loyal to me." So in the second term, the people he picked are first absolutely loyal to him and second highly aligned with his values and ideas. After taking office, Trump's first task is to deal with the "deep state"—the challenge posed by the technical bureaucrats hidden behind the scenes in America. So Trump's team takes a very hardline, very unfriendly stance toward China. On the other hand, however, we note that the first "shockwave" does not seem to be aimed at China, which again proves Trump's unpredictability. We assumed that once Trump took office, China–US relations would be the first to suffer, yet his first shot was aimed at Canada, striking his best neighbor and friend.

But that does not mean China–US relations will move toward a thorough détente. His invitation to President Xi to talk by phone and to China's national leader to attend his inauguration seemed to send some friendly signals. But I do not think China can therefore rest easy; the current signs of easing are somewhat deceptive. Trump is highly flexible. The reason he sends friendly signals is that he understands the United States cannot strike out in all directions at once; there are many urgent problems for the United States to solve right now—the Russia–Ukraine conflict, the Middle East, and so on—while China–US relations are a long-term issue. So I think that if Trump does adopt somewhat softer means toward China, it will only be a short-term phenomenon. The posture Trump is showing at present suggests something like "first pacify the interior before resisting the exterior"; we must not be lulled into negligence and need to be fully prepared to contend with the Trump administration.

Jodie Wen: There are many structural contradictions between China and the United States that cannot be resolved, so strategically we still need to take them seriously. Further, our self-understanding at the strategic and tactical levels matters a great deal—for example, how to deal with Trump's cabinet. This time we saw Vice President Han Zheng meet with US Vice President Vance. I think both sides hope to build a smooth communication framework between China and the United States and to promote direct contact. In his inaugural address Trump said he would take strong measures to make America solve the inflation problem, especially soaring prices. How much of what he says do you think he can deliver? Are you optimistic about his prospects for solving the domestic economic problems?

Li Wei: I am not optimistic. America's economic problems are extremely complex and absolutely cannot be solved by one president, or within a short period. Trump's claim that he will solve inflation is more political bragging, consistent with his usual style, than a practical economic strategy—though he does indeed put solving inflation in a very important position, which is correct. As for why Biden and the Democrats suffered such a crushing defeat this time, there are three main reasons: first, the economic and inflation problem; second, ineffective implementation of immigration policy; and third, overemphasizing political correctness while ignoring the real needs of society, which provoked a backlash. Together these three problems led the broad American public to abandon the Democratic Party. On the economic problem specifically, inflation is the core. In recent years, especially during and after the pandemic, the prices of everyday goods for American households nearly doubled, or more. The inflation the United States now faces is very rare even in its history; it directly hit Americans' standard of living and became the issue of greatest concern to the public.

In his inaugural address Trump proposed two means of solving inflation. First, he advocates cutting government spending, arguing that the main cause of inflation during the Democrats' years in office was excessive government spending, and to that end he proposed establishing a Department of Government Efficiency to deal with redundancy and waste in the US government. I agree with this view of Trump's: the Democratic administration spent lavishly, partly because of its belief in "big government," which emphasizes providing broad support to the public through social security programs. In addition, to respond to the pandemic the government handed out large subsidies to the public, and the Biden administration's industrial policy aimed at reviving manufacturing also required huge investment. These measures put too much money into circulation in the market and thus triggered inflation.

The second solution Trump proposed is to lower energy prices for American businesses. This plan strikes me as very interesting, but also highly challenging. He plans to massively develop America's fossil energy resources, rebuild the United States as a major energy producer, and thereby lower energy prices and curb rising prices. However, this strategy may be a product driven by traditional energy interest groups, and it runs counter to the current global trend of vigorously developing clean energy; the difficulty of implementing it should not be underestimated.

Overall, I think that although these two methods Trump proposes have some pertinence, neither addresses the problem at its root, and they may even create new troubles. First, cutting government spending means Trump plans to further reduce subsidies for reshoring manufacturing and for industrial policy, and may even involve cuts to the US government's health insurance programs. This will inevitably provoke new social conflicts. Around health insurance, the United States has already formed a very large interest group. Touching their cheese is very dangerous. At the same time, the United States also plans to increase defense spending, because Trump has always been keen on developing the military industry. Yet on the one hand he wants to cut government spending and taxes, and on the other he wants to increase defense and military spending—a contradiction that is hard to reconcile in policy and almost impossible to resolve. Second, a fall in energy prices will not happen overnight. Large-scale development of oil and gas resources takes a great deal of time and resources, and its feasibility in terms of time is not high. In addition, Trump proposes a substantial increase in tariffs, but that may push inflation higher still, running counter to his goal of solving inflation.

So we see that there are many contradictions in Trump's economic policy. The root of these contradictions may lie in the fact that the overall level of economic understanding among his cabinet members or staff team is not high—though more importantly it lies in the man himself: Trump is not a seasoned statesman but someone who keeps making choices by personal instinct. His political instinct, which exceeds that of ordinary people, helps him win elections, but it may not help him govern the country. Overall, I do not believe Trump can effectively solve the many domestic challenges on the economic front; he may even make the US economy worse.

Jodie Wen: What Professor Li just said differs somewhat from my earlier expectations. The reason I remain optimistic is that Trump is a businessman. Since as a businessman he could build the "Trump empire" so successfully and become president of the United States, I think he may have the ability to achieve results in managing the national economy that traditional politicians could not.

Li Wei: I think your guess has some merit; after all, Trump is a businessman—but he is not a particularly successful businessman. If we look at Trump's biographies, we find that he is actually a gambler. In the United States, if you want to judge whether a businessman is successful, you can refer to his standing on Wall Street. Many of the truly very successful Wall Street magnates do not think much of Trump; his achievements in business amount to nothing. During Trump's first term, one could even say that the elite of the entire Wall Street opposed and despised him. That is one point I want to add. In addition, we can observe that the best period for the US economy was not when a businessman was in office; the best period in US economic history was the 1990s, under Clinton. Clinton was not a businessman, yet the US economy was strong overall during his tenure. So to develop the US economy, it is not enough for the president himself to have a good economic mind; more importantly, he needs to appoint a group of professional economic bureaucrats. Economic problems today are very complex, and solving them cannot be done overnight. Including the development of cryptocurrency that Trump is pushing, I think Trump has not thought it through carefully. Although he has made a lot of money, it has done lasting damage to the US financial system and credit system, and will very likely leave a mess behind.

Jodie Wen: During the Clinton period, the US domestic economy was indeed very prosperous. Historically, the Democrats have often been more experienced and more accomplished than the Republicans in the economic field, so we cannot assume that Trump's economic policies will produce unique achievements merely because he is a businessman. Also, Musk has been appointed in the Trump 2.0 era as head of the Department of Government Efficiency. I would like to hear your professional analysis of the future vision of the Department of Government Efficiency that Trump has established, and of how far his relationship with Musk will go. In particular, what impact will these two seemingly "sweet" partners have on the US economy in the future?

Li Wei: Some time ago I made a special study of Biden's farewell address. Generally speaking, presidents sometimes speak more candidly when leaving office. I think that this time Biden's farewell address raised a very important concept—that the United States must be wary of a "tech-industrial complex." The concept was modeled on Eisenhower, who warned America at his departure to beware of the "military-industrial complex." Those military-industrial complexes hijacked US foreign policy; the Cold War context was precisely what made US foreign policy serve America's military-industrial interest groups. This time Biden proposed that America beware of its public policy being "captured" by tech elites, which is very interesting. As Ms. Wen just mentioned, the Musk–Trump combination is highly symbolic: it represents a "marriage" between Silicon Valley and Washington. In the past we paid more attention to the relationship between Wall Street and Washington, because the United States has a powerful financial complex, and US public policy and government decisions are indirectly controlled by Wall Street. We used to think these tech elites would have a lot of "backbone"—as high-tech intellectuals mastering cutting-edge technology, they might not favor a figure like Trump. But now Silicon Valley's tech elites have collectively "defected" to Trump; this shift reveals a new trend: the union of the tech field with political power may be reshaping America's political and economic landscape.

Why would Silicon Valley and California, once part of the Democrats' base, collectively turn to Trump? I am still doing research on this, but I can offer a preliminary conclusion: Trump plans to "deregulate" in the tech field, especially artificial intelligence. I am very worried about the negative consequences AI may bring—it may be the greatest disaster facing humanity in the 21st century. During the Biden administration, the United States already imposed many regulatory measures on AI development, especially in the digital economy, to prevent negative effects on the humanistic spirit, ethics and morality, and many social fields, and tech elites and the financial world were very dissatisfied with the Democratic administration's regulatory measures. The AI "moat" the Democrats built was in fact very necessary, but I overestimated the "conscience and morality" of Silicon Valley elites. These tech elites—the "STEM guys"—are mostly eccentric, self-opinionated, full of faith in "technology as omnipotent," extremely lacking in humanistic cultivation, and lacking any sense of awe toward human society. We are very disappointed in them, and I am very much afraid that they are leading humanity into a new abyss.

Musk is close to Trump, and the two are quite aligned in their ideas. If the two of them did not hold such enormous power, they would be the kind of people ordinary people find very unlikeable. Musk's interpersonal relationships are terrible, and few people genuinely like him; Trump, in private, is even more so. The two of them coming together may spark enormous creativity, but both lack a sense of awe toward certain things. My students and I have been tracking and studying Musk; we deeply respect his technological talent and his extraordinary intuition for technology and business. Yet Musk himself does not respect certain values and rules, so their combination may do great harm to society. The two of them coming together may be because they share certain beliefs, and so they will overturn some of the existing social order.

Jodie Wen: Trump believes that in recent years American manufacturing has been severely hit, with many manufacturing jobs and industries gradually moving to other countries, especially China. At the same time, Southeast Asia, with its cost advantages and industrial clusters, has also become an important base for global manufacturing, attracting a large amount of manufacturing back to Asia. In his inaugural address Trump stressed that the United States will again become a manufacturing powerhouse, and that the United States has the world's largest oil and gas reserves, which provides a solid material foundation and confidence for reviving manufacturing. So, do you think that within the next four years the United States can achieve its expected goals in reviving manufacturing?

Li Wei: I think that since the Obama period, successive US administrations have begun working to revive manufacturing, but the process is full of challenges. The decline of American manufacturing and the immigration problem together constitute the two fundamental problems of American society today. If these two problems are not properly solved, the United States will face extremely severe challenges, even crises, in the 21st century.

There is no need to dwell on immigration: Anglo-Saxon whites are gradually becoming a minority. The decline of manufacturing, meanwhile, is the fundamental cause of growing social division in the United States; it directly caused the decline of the American middle class. For the past 200 years the United States has taken pride in being a middle-class society, and a healthy electoral system must also be built on the foundation of a dominant middle class. Today, the United States has already lost the head start in manufacturing in the first decade of the 21st century. After the 2008 financial crisis broke out, both the US and Japanese economies fell into stagnation. This further highlighted the urgency of reviving manufacturing. The first decade of the 21st century happened to be the decade of China's accession to the WTO, a period that also became the key stage of the rapid rise of Chinese manufacturing. Yet some American strategic elites believe that China's WTO accession was an important cause of the decline of American manufacturing. They point out that it was precisely in this period that Chinese manufacturing expanded rapidly while American manufacturing gradually declined. 2010 became an important turning point—China surpassed the United States that year to become the world's largest manufacturing country. That event has profound significance, marking a major shift in the global manufacturing landscape.

The United States began to realize the seriousness of this problem in the Obama period. As a representative of the traditional establishment elite, Obama faced many constraints and found it hard to propose a genuinely anti-liberal solution. He tried to respond to the challenge through policies such as promoting the reshoring of manufacturing, but these efforts did not fundamentally reverse the decline of American manufacturing. The reason is that economic globalization is essentially liberal in character, and both the Democratic Party as a whole and Obama personally belong largely to the liberal camp. Although they recognized the seriousness of the problem, they found it hard to find the right direction. For example, under Obama the United States was still pushing high-standard free trade further through the TPP and TTIP; although China was excluded, the American strategic community still could not give up the idea of free trade—and if the United States continued to pursue free trade, American manufacturing would have no way out. Robert Lighthizer saw this very clearly.

In his first term Trump tried to solve the manufacturing problem by imposing tariffs, but the effect was limited. The Biden administration also proposed a series of domestic and foreign policies aimed at systematically solving the manufacturing problem, such as promoting the return of semiconductor companies like TSMC and developing new energy industries. These measures had a certain effect, but Biden was left with too little time, and a fundamental transformation could hardly be achieved in the short term. Reshoring manufacturing is a long-term process requiring more time and sustained effort; history did not give him enough time to complete the task before passing the baton to Trump. Yet at present it is still hard for us to see how Trump will solve the manufacturing problem. Apart from raising tariffs, he has not put forward a systematic solution. Clearly, the United States faces enormous difficulties in saving manufacturing again, mainly because of two problems: first, the domestic manufacturing ecosystem in the United States has collapsed across the board, and Boeing is the most typical microcosm of American manufacturing; second, America's manufacturing culture has been destroyed, and Americans are less willing to work in manufacturing as industrial workers. Lighthizer pointed out in his book: "Every country becomes great because of production; no country becomes great because of consumption." That sentence hit the core of the problem. Yet the reality is that some of Trump's current moves, such as developing cryptocurrency, seem to lean more toward making quick money than focusing on the long-term development of manufacturing. How to get Americans accustomed again to developing manufacturing and earning "slow money" is a huge challenge for the United States. We will not draw a conclusion yet; let us first observe whether Trump can propose a genuinely workable approach.

Jodie Wen: In this address Trump very clearly set out the idea of expanding energy production. What shock and impact will this have on oil prices in the global energy market?

Li Wei: 2019 happened to be the year when China–US relations faced the most challenges. I went to Texas for field research, preparing for a possible second Trump term. At the time the pandemic had not yet broken out, and we could not foresee its profound impact on the global economy and on China–US relations. So, judging from the situation at the time, we basically thought Trump was likely to be re-elected. Under the assumption of his re-election, the China–US phase-one trade agreement was at a critical moment close to being concluded. So we predicted that the two sides would successfully sign the agreement and, on that basis, work together to reverse the imbalance in China–US trade. As the world's largest manufacturing country and one of the largest energy consumers, China had a strong interest in importing shale gas and shale oil from the United States on a large scale. We believed the prospects for this import plan were very broad. And one of the focal points of traditional energy development in the United States is precisely Texas, which was an important reason we chose to conduct research there.

From China's point of view, we are glad to see Trump develop traditional energy. His emphasis on traditional energy means that China will have greater room for development and advantage in clean energy. Traditional energy technology is already relatively mature; its development and use are more a matter of tapping and selling to market demand. China, as a country relatively scarce in energy, is instead pushed by this external pressure to accelerate the development of clean energy. That is why China has already become world number one in electric vehicles, wind and solar power, and is very likely to reach a leading position in nuclear energy in the future. If the United States increases its development of traditional energy and extracts large amounts of oil and gas, that will bring down prices in the global energy market. This trend makes major energy producers such as Russia, the Middle East and the United States all willing to sell energy to China, which not only enriches China's sources of energy imports but also helps safeguard China's energy security. An important problem the Chinese economy now faces is that energy prices are too high, which directly affects the living costs of businesses and residents. From this angle, increasing import sources and lowering domestic energy prices, thereby lightening the burden on ordinary people, is a good thing for China.

On the other hand, I think Trump may have set off in the wrong direction. There is an extremely key concept here: the "resource curse." We observe that all energy hubs around the world, especially regions that depend on traditional energy, face many challenges in social governance and economic development. It is precisely on the basis of these phenomena that economists proposed the concept of the "resource curse." Trump's large-scale development of traditional energy does not involve much technological progress and may even trap the United States in excessive dependence on traditional energy, so that it loses its way in energy strategy. I think Trump needs to understand this problem deeply, because in the long run his energy strategy may lead the United States into a huge strategic blunder. From China's point of view, however, we actually hope that Trump will continue to develop traditional energy on a large scale.

Jodie Wen: In his inaugural address Trump mentioned that the United States will cancel subsidies for electric vehicles. As you just said, the United States needs to return to traditional manufacturing—that is, it will take a more conservative attitude toward clean energy development in the energy field. For China this is a great advantage.

Li Wei: The United States ending the "Green New Deal" may bring China some pressure in the short term, but China needs to remain unmoved—even to do the opposite. We will further advance the green agenda. On the one hand, we need to improve the ecological environment; large-scale industrialization at home is bringing a huge environmental crisis. We do indeed face enormous environmental pressure, and we must unswervingly pursue the "Green New Deal." On the other hand, the green economy, or the new energy economy, has brought enormous technological development. Traditional energy no longer has much room for technological innovation, while new energy brings technological progress. So we must unswervingly adopt a strategy of doing the opposite of the United States, and even hurry to widen the gap with the United States in clean energy technology and industry.

Jodie Wen: I also understand personally that the United States is in fact very backward in building supporting infrastructure for electric vehicles. Judging by Trump's inaugural address, he probably will not increase technological investment in this area next.

Li Wei: Yes. From this angle, the technological gap between China and the United States in clean energy will widen further. Although the United States has an advantage in artificial intelligence, in some industrial fields China has already developed clean energy and new energy that are more representative than America's. I would like to talk about the Middle East. I think the UAE is an exception worth noting; you have visited the Middle East many times and may understand this better than I do. The UAE is a resource-rich country with large oil and gas reserves. Yet in recent years the UAE government has invested heavily in clean energy, including photovoltaics and solar power generation. In my view, a country as rich in oil and gas as the UAE has already recognized the importance of escaping dependence on traditional energy and is actively putting money into promoting clean energy development. From this angle, I very much admire the efforts of the UAE government. Today the UAE has become a shining star in the Middle East, which is closely related to the foresight of the local government's leaders. That is a humble observation of mine.

Jodie Wen: You just mentioned the UAE and other Gulf states, such as Saudi Arabia—all traditional energy powers. Despite their rich oil and energy reserves, they are still actively developing new energy. In fact, Saudi Arabia's natural environment makes this transition very difficult and painful; it is a huge challenge for the government. For example, in Riyadh, even in a not particularly hot season, waiting for a bus at a bus stop is unbearable. When I was in Riyadh I noticed that there was almost nobody at the bus stops. We visited Saudi Arabia's bus group, where the buses were extremely luxurious, beyond imagination. Even so, many people are still unwilling to choose public transport and to achieve green travel through new energy. Although constrained by natural conditions, the Saudi government is still working hard to promote reform.

You just mentioned that the United States has very large energy reserves, and that whether in technology or resource conditions it is well placed to support new energy development, yet it wants to return to the traditional energy era—which I find puzzling. Perhaps in the short term many US economic figures will look good, but personally I do not think this is wise in the long run.

Li Wei: I very much agree with what you just said. Our judgment of Trump should be twofold: we should recognize that some of Trump's directions are correct, including developing manufacturing, restraining Wall Street's financial power, encouraging high-tech development, and cutting the size of government. Yet in certain areas, I think some of Trump's ideas may not lead the United States in the right direction. The risk of Trump's four years in office ending in enormous failure should not be underestimated, so we should not deify him.

Jodie Wen: What netizens care about most is trade policy and tariff prices; during the campaign they mentioned many times that they would launch a tariff war. I am more focused on the additional 10 percent tariff the United States has proposed on China. Do you think there will be genuine follow-through and more concrete action on this front going forward?

Li Wei: Tariff policy is one of the core elements of Trump's economic policy. In his inaugural address he specifically mentioned former US President William McKinley, whom he considers very great. McKinley promoted the development of American manufacturing through a high-tariff policy, laying a solid foundation for America's second industrial revolution. That industrial revolution was achieved not against the background of free trade but against the background of trade protection, and this historical experience has had a profound influence on Trump's policy thinking. Trump says the United States does not bear international responsibilities and puts America first, but that does not mean the United States will withdraw entirely from international affairs. On the contrary, he hopes to score some achievements in international affairs through more strategic engagement. McKinley in his time promoted American expansionism and the Open Door policy, which is regarded as the watershed marking America's transition from 19th-century isolationism to internationalism, and the beginning of the United States as an emerging imperialist power. Trump's mention of McKinley may hint that he longs to replicate McKinley's achievements and reshape America's international standing through tough means. I observe that Trump will most likely use the tariff tool again in his second term to achieve his policy goals, especially international policy goals. I think the biggest difference from the first term is that tariffs are not only a means to an end but may become the end itself. Since American manufacturing struggles to compete with other countries, without building the "wall" the process of reviving American manufacturing may be further delayed.

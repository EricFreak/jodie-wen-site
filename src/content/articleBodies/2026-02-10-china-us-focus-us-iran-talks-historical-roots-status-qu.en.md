On February 6, the United States and Iran held indirect talks in Muscat, the capital of Oman. The U.S. side was led by Middle East Special Envoy Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, Trump's son-in-law, while the Iranian side was headed by Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi. Intriguingly, and unlike in previous rounds, Cooper, commander of U.S. Central Command—the most senior U.S. military commander in the Middle East—took part in the talks as a member of the U.S. delegation. Why have the Oman talks drawn so much attention, what are the historical roots of the U.S.-Iran dispute over the nuclear issue, and what are the prospects for the negotiations? This article attempts to answer these questions.

## What Is the Background and What Are the Historical Roots?

These talks centered mainly on the Iranian nuclear issue. For a long time, that issue has been the core sticking point in bilateral relations between the two countries and a key variable affecting the Middle East security situation. Iran regards the possession of nuclear capability as a symbol of national strength. Beginning in the 1950s, Iran developed nuclear technology with the help of Western countries including the United States, Germany and France; on March 5, 1957, representatives of the two countries signed in Tehran an agreement on cooperative research and the peaceful use of atomic energy.

The dramatic turning point came with the outbreak of the Islamic Revolution in Iran in 1979. The newly established Islamic regime adopted an anti-American stance, and the hostage crisis at the U.S. Embassy in Tehran that same year directly led the two countries to sever diplomatic relations in 1980. These events marked the transformation of the United States and Iran from strategic allies into adversaries, prompting the United States to adjust its position on Iran's nuclear policy and begin opposing Iran's development of nuclear technology.

From 1979 to 2002, Iran was in the early stage of developing its nuclear program. On August 14, 2002, the Iranian opposition group National Council of Resistance of Iran (NCRI) claimed that Iran had conducted secret nuclear activities without the consent of the International Atomic Energy Agency, including building nuclear facilities near Natanz and Arak. From then on, Iran's nuclear program turned into the "Iranian nuclear issue" and became a focus of international attention.

Starting in 2002, the international community, represented by the International Atomic Energy Agency, began a dialogue with Iran on the "Iranian nuclear issue." On February 4, 2006, a special session of the IAEA Board of Governors referred the issue to the United Nations Security Council. The resolution held that it was "necessary" for Iran to suspend activities related to uranium enrichment and to reconsider the construction of the Arak heavy water reactor. On June 6, the United States, Britain, Russia, China, France and Germany—the six parties on the Iranian nuclear issue—presented Iran with a framework agreement encouraging it to halt its uranium enrichment program indefinitely. With this, the United States became an important party to the Iranian nuclear issue, which also began to evolve into a key problem in U.S.-Iran relations.

On July 14, 2015, under President Obama's leadership, the United States, together with China, France, Britain, Russia and Germany, reached a political understanding with Iran on the key issues of a comprehensive long-term agreement on its nuclear program and signed the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA). From a diplomatic perspective, the conclusion of the JCPOA represented a rare rebuilding of trust in U.S.-Iran relations during the Obama administration. But the good times did not last, and this fragile foundation of mutual trust was soon broken. In 2018, the Trump administration unilaterally withdrew from the JCPOA and restored economic sanctions on Iran, seeking to force concessions on the nuclear issue by reviving the policy of "maximum pressure." It is fair to say that the U.S. unilateral withdrawal fundamentally undermined the institutional credibility of negotiations and became an important historical root of the current diplomatic impasse between the two countries.

## Why Have the Oman Talks Become a Global Focus?

The Oman talks have drawn global attention for three main reasons. First, the tense and hostile standoff between the two countries had reached a critical point. U.S.-Iran relations had long been on a knife's edge. In the "12-Day Israel-Iran War" in June 2025, the United States used B-2 bombers and more than 30 Tomahawk missiles to strike Iran's three main nuclear sites at Fordow, Isfahan and Natanz, escalating a dispute between two Middle Eastern states into a direct military strike by the United States—an extra-regional power—against Iran, a major regional power. In December 2025, large-scale protests broke out in Iran over rising prices, currency depreciation and other problems, and escalated into riots in some areas, even causing heavy casualties. As the United States kept up its pressure, Trump announced the dispatch of an aircraft carrier strike group to the Middle East, massing in waters around Iran, and the State Department issued a "security alert" requiring U.S. citizens to leave Iran as soon as possible. Tensions between the two sides had reached the point where a single spark could set them off.

Second, Oman's role as a neutral mediator. As a neutral regional mediator, Oman has repeatedly served as a "bridge" in the diplomatic history between the two countries. In 2025, under Omani mediation, the United States and Iran held five rounds of indirect talks on the nuclear issue, but the core differences remained unresolved: the United States demanded that Iran completely abandon uranium enrichment, while Iran held that its right to the peaceful use of nuclear energy is inalienable. After the 12-Day Israel-Iran War broke out, the talks were suspended. Before this round, White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt noted that the meeting had originally been scheduled to take place in Turkey before being moved to Oman. The main reason was that Iran considered Oman more neutral and trustworthy in U.S.-Iran diplomacy; Turkey, though also a major regional player, might bring in more issues and audiences that Iran did not want to address, making the negotiating environment more complicated.

Third, the definition of the core agenda. What has drawn the most attention to these indirect talks is what exactly would be discussed—that is, how the two sides would define the core issues. The United States wanted the agenda to include curbing Iranian nuclear proliferation and ending Iran's support for regional armed groups such as Hamas, Hezbollah and the Houthis in Yemen. Iran's core concerns, however, were the lifting of all U.S. sanctions and the Iranian nuclear issue. Washington held that Iran should not conduct any uranium enrichment activity at all, while Tehran maintained that it has the right to the peaceful use of nuclear energy.

## What Lies Ahead?

The Oman talks went beyond the traditional negotiations over the so-called Iranian nuclear issue and bore on war and peace in the Middle East. According to the latest publicly available information, the talks have "temporarily" concluded and both delegations have returned home. A statement said the talks had created suitable conditions for the two countries to resume diplomatic and technical-level negotiations, but that no substantive agreement had yet been reached.

In essence, the talks were markedly "temporary" and geared toward "crisis management"; they were not an institutional negotiating process that the two sides had planned over a long period and pursued step by step in a relatively relaxed environment. In other words, this was not a patient dialogue grounded in sufficient mutual trust and strategic consensus, but rather an emergency diplomatic attempt forced open against the backdrop of an escalating tense standoff. The United States repeatedly sent tough signals and even publicly threatened that military action against Iran could not be ruled out, abruptly tightening the regional security atmosphere. Under such circumstances, the talks were closer to a stop-loss operation to "avoid an uncontrolled conflict" than to a genuine reshaping of the relationship.

More symbolic still, on the very day the two sides held talks, the U.S. State Department announced that it would impose sanctions on multiple entities, individuals and tankers linked to Iran's oil trade, in order to target Iran's "illicit oil traders" and "shadow fleet." The move showed that the United States had not suspended its tools of pressure because of the talks, but continued to maintain a two-track strategy of "dialogue and sanctions in parallel." The outlook for U.S.-Iran relations is full of uncertainty, but continued dialogue remains the rational choice for avoiding a catastrophic conflict.

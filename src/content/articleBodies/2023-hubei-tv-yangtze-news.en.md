On January 16, Chinese Foreign Minister Qin Gang's visit to Africa came to a successful close. The Chinese foreign minister's first overseas trip of the new year was tightly scheduled and richly productive; it continued the tradition of the Chinese foreign minister's first overseas visit of the year going to Africa for 33 consecutive years, and it further demonstrated the important place of the China-Africa comprehensive strategic and cooperative partnership in China's diplomatic strategy. Yet after the Chinese foreign minister visited Ethiopia last week, German Foreign Minister Annalena Baerbock and French Foreign Minister Catherine Colonna also jointly paid a two-day visit to that East African country. After the Chinese foreign minister concluded his Africa visit, US Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen set off on an 11-day Africa trip. Why did senior European and US officials follow so closely on the heels of the Chinese foreign minister's departure? Yangtze News connects with Jodie Wen, assistant research fellow at the Center for International Security and Strategy (CISS), Tsinghua University, for an in-depth analysis.

Yangtze News: Only a month after the China-Arab Summit concluded, Chinese Foreign Minister Qin Gang visited the headquarters of the Arab League. What signal do you think this sends?

Jodie Wen: Minister Qin Gang's visit to the Arab League headquarters was mainly to convey to the Arab League countries China's willingness to strengthen communication and coordination with Arab countries and deepen cooperation between the two sides. The most important element is implementing the series of outcomes of President Xi Jinping's December visit to Saudi Arabia and his participation in the China-Arab Summit. First, we need to be clear that the first China-Arab Summit was of great significance: it was the largest and highest-level diplomatic event China has held with the Arab world since the founding of New China, and it issued three weighty outcome documents — the Riyadh Declaration of the First China-Arab Summit, the Outline of the Comprehensive Cooperation Plan between China and Arab States, and the Document on Deepening the China-Arab Strategic Partnership for Peace and Development. Second, we can see that during Minister Qin Gang's visit the two sides mainly communicated and coordinated in three areas: security, trade and economic cooperation, and people-to-people and cultural exchange. In the security field, the focus was on implementing the Riyadh Declaration, upholding the international order based on international law and multilateralism, advocating the establishment of a cooperative and sustainable Middle East security architecture, and contributing Chinese wisdom and Chinese solutions to the resolution of regional security issues through the Global Security Initiative. What deserves particular attention is that at the press conference in Cairo, Minister Qin Gang clearly stated that the Palestinian question is the core of the Middle East issue, and that since Biden took office the United States has marginalized the Palestinian question; by making such a statement, Minister Qin Gang shows that China attaches importance to the Palestinian question. In the trade and economic field, the focus was on implementing the Outline of the Comprehensive Cooperation Plan between China and Arab States, which sets out concrete measures for 18 specific areas of cooperation. In the people-to-people field, the focus was on carrying forward the friendly spirit of "mutual assistance, equality and mutual benefit, and inclusiveness and mutual learning" and on strengthening exchanges between Chinese and Arab civilizations.

Yangtze News: Minister Qin Gang's seven-day Africa visit has now concluded successfully and produced a series of important outcomes. Which of these outcomes left the deepest impression on you?

Jodie Wen: On this trip Minister Qin Gang visited five African countries. Although the Chinese foreign minister has had the tradition, since 1991, of making Africa the destination of his first overseas visit of the year for 33 consecutive years, Minister Qin Gang's trip was still of great significance. First, it advanced China's relations with African countries along both the bilateral and the multilateral dimension: Minister Qin Gang not only visited the five African countries of Ethiopia, Gabon, Angola, Benin and Egypt, but also visited the headquarters of the African Union. China's diplomacy holds that "major powers are the key, the periphery is the priority, developing countries are the foundation, and multilateral platforms are the stage." All five countries visited on this Africa trip are developing countries — the foundation on which China's diplomacy rests — while the African Union, as Africa's largest regional cooperation organization, is an important multilateral institution and an important stage for China's diplomatic practice. Second, it advanced China-Africa cooperation across the political, economic and cultural fields. China has been Africa's largest trading partner for the past 13 years, and two-way trade is expected to exceed US$260 billion in 2022. During the COVID-19 pandemic, China carried out extensive anti-epidemic cooperation with African countries including Morocco and Algeria. On this trip Minister Qin Gang also specially attended the completion ceremony of the first headquarters building of the Africa CDC, which was built by China Railway Construction Corporation. In addition, when meeting with Egyptian President Sisi, Minister Qin Gang said that China will continue to develop investment in Egyptian infrastructure projects, including projects related to the Belt and Road Initiative jointly pursued with China.

Yangtze News: The Chinese foreign minister had just finished visiting the five African countries and the African Union headquarters when the US treasury secretary could hardly wait to begin her 11-day Africa trip. This is obviously no coincidence. How do you view this arrangement by the US side?

Jodie Wen: One month before Yellen's visit, the Biden administration had just held the US-Africa Leaders Summit in Washington, receiving 49 African leaders and announcing that it would provide US$55 billion in assistance to Africa over the next three years, mainly for economic, health and security support. By sending the treasury secretary to Africa this time, the Biden administration is sending a very clear signal: it hopes to strengthen ties with Africa and expand the areas of US-Africa economic cooperation. Since taking office, the Biden administration has stepped up its diplomatic efforts toward Africa. Secretary of State Blinken has visited Africa twice, and the administration has issued a strategy report on Africa. The main reason is that during Trump's term the United States attached extremely little importance to Africa, causing a sharp decline in American influence in Africa. For many years, by contrast, the Chinese government has maintained stable political, trade and economic, and cultural relations with Africa — relations that do not change with shifts in the US attitude toward Africa. Since 2000, China-Africa summits have been held once every three years. According to statistics, between 2009 and 2021 China-Africa trade exceeded approximately US$207 billion, with average annual growth of more than 37 percent, while China's direct investment stood at US$2.59 billion, with average annual growth of about 10 percent. US Treasury Secretary Yellen's trip is a guard against, and a warning about, China's influence in Africa.

Yangtze News: Why have Western countries, including the United States, Germany and France, been constantly stepping up their diplomatic offensives toward Africa in recent years? And why do they try every means to fabricate all kinds of strange arguments in an attempt to sow discord in China-Africa relations?

Jodie Wen: First, Africa, as the world's youngest continent, possesses abundant energy reserves such as oil and a vibrant economic market, and is indeed an important region of international attention. Second, from the perspective of the Western countries themselves, Africa is vital to their diplomatic influence and economic interests. The United States has been mentioned above; take France as an example. France has historically been a country with enormous influence in Africa — many African countries were once French colonies, and to this day French remains the most widely used language in Africa — and Macron announced earlier that he would make Africa one of the priorities of his second five-year term. As Europe's largest economy, Germany's stepped-up diplomatic offensive toward Africa is mainly driven by economic interests. Affected by the Ukraine crisis, Germany has been seeking to reduce its dependence on Russian natural gas, and it hopes to expand cooperation with Africa in infrastructure and in new energy fields such as liquefied natural gas; Senegal, Nigeria and other such African countries are all regarded by Germany as countries with relatively large investment potential. By contrast, China's growing influence on the African continent makes Western countries uneasy. Western countries, whether European or American, have made demonizing China's presence in Africa a core issue in their political and media discourse. Take the "debt trap" claim: according to a report released based on World Bank data, only 12 percent of African governments' external debt is owed to China, while the share held by Western private lenders reaches 35 percent. So it is very clear to us that, unless they demonize China, Western countries seem to find it hard to obtain the support they expect from African leaders and elites.

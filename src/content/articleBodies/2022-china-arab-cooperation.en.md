From December 7 to 10, Chinese President Xi Jinping attended the First China–Arab States Summit and the China–Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) Summit and paid a state visit to Saudi Arabia. As the largest and highest-level diplomatic undertaking China has organized toward the Arab world since the founding of the People's Republic of China, the smooth convening of the China–Arab States Summit is widely regarded as holding special significance as a milestone and an epoch-making event.

## New Developments in China–Arab Cooperation

Xi's trip to Saudi Arabia produced remarkable results, whether at the multilateral level of China–Arab and China–GCC relations or at the bilateral level of China–Saudi relations.

The two sides issued the Riyadh Declaration of the First China–Arab States Summit, agreeing unanimously to make every effort to build a China–Arab community with a shared future for the new era and to advance the "eight major joint actions" in eight areas, including support for development, food security, energy security, dialogue among civilizations, and security and stability. At the China–GCC Summit, China decided to advance cooperation with GCC states in five priority areas: energy, financial investment, scientific and technological innovation, aerospace, and language and culture. The two sides also agreed to strengthen technological cooperation in the 5G and 6G fields. China–Saudi relations entered an entirely new stage as King Salman met with Xi and personally signed the first China–Saudi Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Agreement. In addition, the leaders of the two countries agreed to align the Belt and Road Initiative with Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030; the two sides issued a joint statement and signed 20 cooperation outcome documents.

China–Arab cooperation did not come about overnight. In 1956, Egypt became the first Arab state to establish diplomatic relations with New China, opening the process of establishing diplomatic ties between China and Arab states. The foundation and underlying character of early China–Arab exchanges were China's active support for the Arab national liberation movement and its opposition to US and British hegemonism in the Middle East. After China's reform and opening up, Arab states became an important export market and source of foreign exchange for China, and a major partner for China's labor exports and overseas project contracting. In 2001, China joined the WTO; with complementary trade advantages, China–Arab cooperation in energy and other fields accelerated across the board. Before this China–Arab States Summit, a report released by the research institute of China's Ministry of Commerce showed that from 2004 to 2021, total imports and exports between China and the 22 Arab states grew from US$36.7 billion to US$330.2 billion, and that China had been the largest trading partner of Arab states for two consecutive years. In 2013, China became Saudi Arabia's largest trading partner. In 2021, the Chinese market accounted for 27 percent of Saudi Arabia's total crude oil exports, and bilateral trade between the two countries reached US$87.3 billion.

## Strategic Considerations of China and the Arab World

Behind every political act lies an underlying logic. For a long time, the Arab world—because of power-based interference by extra-regional great powers, highly uneven economic development, and intricate sectarian rivalries—remained fragmented in the global configuration of power and never attained strength and status commensurate with its population and territory. During the Cold War, Arab states were forced to choose sides between the US and Soviet camps and were constantly and deeply affected by geopolitical conflict. Entering the 21st century, after the shock of the "Arab Spring" and the wars launched by the United States in the region, traditional Arab political heavyweights such as Egypt, Syria, and Iraq saw their overall strength decline. By contrast, Gulf states represented by Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Qatar rapidly accumulated wealth through oil and natural gas, actively carried out domestic reforms, and projected their influence across the region and the world.

Among them, Saudi Arabia, as the only Arab member of the G20, has been the most conspicuous. In 2016, Saudi Arabia put forward Vision 2030, setting three major goals for the kingdom: to become the heart of the Arab and Islamic worlds, a global investment powerhouse, and a hub linking Asia, Europe, and Africa. To achieve these three goals, Saudi Arabia needs to break away from its previous single-track economic structure, while China has advantages in new energy, infrastructure, 5G, and other digital economy fields—one could say that the two have formed a degree of strategic complementarity. The US–Saudi alliance built on "oil for security" also has fairly deep foundations, but in recent years the two countries have seen continuous frictions over human rights, oil prices, and other issues. Arab states represented by Saudi Arabia believe that they "need not put all their eggs in the American basket" and should pursue a "balancing diplomacy" between China and the United States.

China's decision to make this Middle East trip the finale of this year's head-of-state diplomacy also demonstrates the high importance China attaches to developing relations with Arab states. Concepts such as "harmony is precious" run through the core values of China's diplomacy. China and the Arab world are both ancient civilizations, and both have gone through national awakening and liberation struggles. For a long time, the Middle East has lacked mechanisms for security dialogue and platforms for multilateral dialogue. China has proposed the Global Security Initiative and a new architecture for Middle East security, which have been welcomed by Arab states. China's Middle East policy stems from the internal impetus of its own development; China needs to uphold the external environment required for peaceful development. Arab states represented by Saudi Arabia are important countries in China's Belt and Road Initiative and important markets for Chinese consumer goods and high-tech products.

For a long time, China was not an important participant in Middle East affairs; since World War II, the United States has long played a leading role in Middle East affairs. However, with the United States' strategic retrenchment in the Middle East, the deepening of regional states' cooperation with China in trade and economics, and the development needs of Chinese industry going global, China's cooperation with the Middle East has grown increasingly close in recent years—something that has also drawn "attention" from the United States.

In July this year, before his Middle East trip, Biden published an op-ed in The Washington Post stating that "the main purpose of his trip to Saudi Arabia was to continue to exert influence in the Middle East and to repair relations with Saudi Arabia," and during the visit he also stated that "the United States will never allow China and Russia to occupy the vacuum created after the United States leaves the Middle East."

However, the advancement and deepening of China's cooperation with the Middle East cannot be interpreted simply and one-sidedly as "China will fill the vacuum left by the United States." China does not seek to compete with the United States for so-called spheres of influence in the Middle East; instead, it advocates expanding bilateral and multilateral relations under the principles of "mutual respect and mutual non-interference," and holds that countries with different cultures and different social systems can fully tolerate one another, learn from one another, and achieve common development and prosperity.

Viewing the Middle East entirely through the narrative of great-power competition may be biased; an important change that deserves attention is that the strategic autonomy of regional powers represented by Saudi Arabia is steadily strengthening.

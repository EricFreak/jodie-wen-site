BEIJING, March 3 (China News Service, reporter Zhu Yanjing) — On February 28 local time, the United States and Israel launched a large-scale attack on Iran, and Iran's Supreme Leader Khamenei was killed in the strike. Iran then launched fierce retaliation against U.S. and Israeli targets, and the situation in the Middle East escalated sharply...

Quite a few analyses hold that this strike on Iran represents “Israel lighting the fire and the United States pouring on the oil”; other views argue that Israel “dragged the United States in.” Are the strategic goals of the United States and Israel on the Iran issue completely consistent? Was the lightning shift from “negotiation” to “military strike” a temporary adjustment in U.S. Middle East policy, or a tactical strategy?

“We cannot say entirely that Israel dragged the United States in,” Jodie Wen, a postdoctoral research fellow at the Center for International Security and Strategy (CISS) at Tsinghua University, said in an interview with China News Service, noting that the United States and Israel share broadly similar strategic goals on part of the Iran issue.

Wen analyzed that, first, both the United States and Israel treat Iran as a threat in the Middle East.

Iran lies to the northeast of Israel. To Israel's south are Hamas and the Houthis in Yemen, while Hezbollah's forces in Lebanon are positioned to its north.

In Wen's view, Israel's primary goal is to safeguard its own regional security, and this is also where an important U.S. interest in the Middle East lies.

“Beyond that, according to U.S. and Israeli assessments, the enrichment level of the uranium Iran currently possesses has already reached 60 percent,” Wen said, adding that this makes the United States and Israel feel extremely endangered. They worry that once Iran possesses nuclear weapons, it would pose a major threat to Israel's security in the Middle East.

Wen said the third core goal of the United States and Israel is to contain Iran's growing strength in the Middle East and prevent it from disrupting the U.S.-Israeli security arrangements in the region.

So what kind of security architecture would serve U.S. and Israeli interests?

Wen said that during his first term, U.S. President Donald Trump pushed through the signing of the Abraham Accords, promoting the normalization of Israel's relations with the UAE and other Arab countries. In his second term, Trump also wants to push for normalization between Israel and Saudi Arabia, but this goal has still not been achieved. The core demand of the Trump administration is to build a regional security architecture in the Middle East that better serves U.S. and Israeli interests.

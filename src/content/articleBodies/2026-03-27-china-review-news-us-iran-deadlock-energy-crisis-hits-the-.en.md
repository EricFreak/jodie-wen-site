China Review News, Beijing, March 27 (Reporter Chen Siyuan) — The US-Iran conflict continues to escalate, and the differences between the two sides over core issues such as the terms for ending the war and the safety of nuclear facilities have become stark. Dr. Jodie Wen, a scholar at the Center for International Security and Strategy (CISS) at Tsinghua University, said in an interview with China Review News that although the conflict has lasted for some time, both sides are still showing a willingness to talk, and that the current deadlock ultimately comes down to the fact that the conditions each side has put forward are unacceptable to the other. She added that the energy crisis triggered by the Middle East conflict will deal a blow to the artificial intelligence industry.

## The Two Sides Cannot Agree? Each Finds the Other's "Asking Price" Unacceptable

On March 23, Trump said he had held "productive" talks with Iran, and Iran immediately denied that any negotiations between the two countries existed. According to other reports, on March 24 the United States handed Iran, through Pakistan, a 15-point plan aimed at ending the war; Iran rejected the US ceasefire proposal and put forward five conditions of its own for a ceasefire.

Wen believes that although the conflict has lasted for some time, at this juncture both Washington and Tehran still have the will to talk. The phenomenon of each side talking past the other stems fundamentally from the fact that the demands they have put forward are mutually unacceptable. As for the five conditions Iran has proposed for a ceasefire, Wen believes that apart from "achieving a halt or temporary suspension of the war," the other conditions are difficult for the United States to accept.

Looking back at what triggered the war, Wen analyzes that the US-Israeli action had three main objectives: first, to strike Iran's nuclear facilities; second, to push for regime change in Iran; and third, to contain the "Shiite arc" Iran has formed in the region, so as to safeguard Israel's regional security.

Wen further points out, however, that the United States and Israel start from different premises in their Iran policies. America's core interests in the Middle East are relatively stable: first, upholding Israel's security; second, ensuring the steady flow of energy (oil); and third, preventing and combating terrorism. Israel, by contrast, pursues a more extreme "absolute security," seeking to eliminate any force that poses a threat to its survival, including Hamas and the sphere of Shiite influence backed by Iran.

Wen says the Gulf states prefer a regional balance and do not want either Israel or Iran to become excessively strong, which also complicates the situation.

## The US "Carrot and Stick" Strategy and the Growing Need for Navigability in the Strait of Hormuz

At present, on the one hand, the United States is sending additional forces to the Middle East, including a Marine Expeditionary Unit and the Army's 82nd Airborne Division, with several warships redeployed at the same time; the timing of some deployments even overlaps with the so-called "deadline" set by the US side. On the other hand, the US side is working through Pakistan and other countries to mediate indirect talks with Iran.

Wen notes that this is the "carrot and stick" strategy the United States has consistently pursued: military deployments to exert deterrence on the one hand, and dialogue as an inducement on the other, projecting a posture of seeking peace. In Wen's view, however, the purpose of this US military deployment differs from earlier ones: beyond strategic deterrence, a more prominent goal at present is to ensure the security of the Strait of Hormuz and related shipping lanes.

Wen says that although the United States has achieved energy self-sufficiency through the shale gas revolution and no longer depends on Middle East energy supplies, about 80 percent of the oil transported through the Strait of Hormuz goes to Asian countries such as China, Japan and South Korea. Japan and South Korea, as US allies, have energy-supply dependencies that are directly tied to America's regional strategic layout. At the same time, the interconnectedness of the global economy means that the impact of a sharp rise in oil prices will gradually be transmitted to the United States, which is also why the United States cannot stand aside.

She points out that if navigation through the Strait of Hormuz is affected for a prolonged period, it will deal a major blow to global oil prices and international energy supplies, and pressure from US energy giants and from the international community is pushing the United States to devote more resources to protecting navigability through the strait.

## The Potential Impact of the Energy Crisis: The AI Industry Takes a Hit

Wen points out that the artificial intelligence sector occupies an important place in today's global economy. The AI industry's demand for computing power makes it highly dependent on energy, and if energy shortages persist over the long term, this will deal a huge blow to the global layout of computing capacity.

Wen particularly mentions that Abu Dhabi, as the world's third pole of artificial intelligence alongside China and the United States, has abundant energy and computing resources, and major US technology companies such as Microsoft all have important branches there. Iran has previously stated that if attacked by the United States, it would attack facilities associated with US companies overseas, which would inflict even greater losses on the AI industry. In addition, TSMC in Taiwan plays an important role in the global semiconductor and artificial intelligence industrial chains, and instability in the Middle East could further aggravate Taiwan's energy crisis, producing a chain reaction across the global AI industry.

The Cold War was one of the most far-reaching historical events of the second half of the twentieth century. Great-power rivalry, regional conflict, ideological confrontation—many of the problems we face today can in fact be traced back to that long Cold War era.

As major shifts have unfolded in the international landscape, the term "Cold War" has once again begun appearing frequently in public view and in academic discussion. We need to return to the historical scene of that time in order to better understand today's complex world.

In today's article, we revisit the reflections of Wang Jisi, author of *The Story of the Cold War* and professor at the School of International Studies at Peking University, on "the Cold War and us." In his answers, Professor Wang traces, from a historical perspective, the place and particularity of the Cold War in the twentieth-century international order; he discusses the Cold War's profound impact on international relations, ideological conflict and the geopolitical landscape; and he further explores the experience and lessons the Cold War has left behind, offering an important reference point for understanding the increasingly frequent talk of a "new Cold War" today. The interviewer, Jodie Wen, is a postdoctoral fellow at the Center for International Security and Strategy (CISS), Tsinghua University.

## 01 Living Through the Cold War

Jodie Wen: Recently, Professor Wang's book *The Story of the Cold War*, based on the Vistopia audio program, has been formally published. What was your original intention and thinking when you made such an audio program, and then organized the audio program into such a thick book?

Wang Jisi: My thinking at the time was very simple. I ran into a friend I had never actually met in person, Liu Ruilin—Ms. Liu Ruilin is the founder of Imaginist and Vistopia—and the two of us sat together chatting at a friend's home. She said, "What are you working on now?" I said, "It's the pandemic, I'm not doing much." She said, "Could you do an audio program?" I said, "Sure, on what?" She proposed the Cold War. Later I thought about it: I really do have a connection to the Cold War, I am interested in it, and I have published a little bit on the subject, so that is how it came about.

When I made the audio program, I wrote down every single word in all seriousness and read from a script. So it could quickly become a book. Of course, there were still many twists and turns along the way. So first of all I need to thank Imaginist and Vistopia, and also some of my collaborators. An old man like me, together with several young people—we worked together, and they put in a great deal of effort. I won't name them one by one; they are in the afterword of the book. In short, thank you very much for all the support.

Jodie Wen: I learned from the book that you were born in the 1940s and lived through the entire outbreak of the Cold War. In your view, why did the Cold War happen?

Wang Jisi: As for how the Cold War came about—first, the two great powers, the United States and the Soviet Union, had already come into conflict before the Cold War even began. I explain this in my book, so I won't go into detail here. I would like to take this opportunity to talk about how I personally came to have a relationship with the Cold War.

I was born in 1948, and the Cold War began in 1947. In the 1950s, we talked every day about "overthrowing American imperialism."

From 1968 to 1978, at the height of the Cold War, I was sent to the countryside. In 1968 I went to a pastoral area in Inner Mongolia as a sent-down youth; in early 1969, Sino-Soviet relations began to tense, and there were rumors that China and the Soviet Union were going to war, and in the end a war of no small size did break out at Zhenbao Island. While we were in Inner Mongolia, we truly felt that war was coming. After that came Nixon's visit to China in 1972. My years in primary school, junior high school and senior high school were filled with these events.

Something that sets this book somewhat apart from others is that I expanded the scope of the Cold War. In most people's understanding, the Cold War was a matter between the United States and the Soviet Union, but it was in fact also very much a Chinese matter—it was connected from the very beginning. Later, almost everything that happened anywhere in the world was connected to the Cold War, whether in Latin America or in Africa, yet there are not many books on these aspects. I have tried hard to bring them together with the Cold War.

## 02 The Legacy of the Cold War

Jodie Wen: Professor Wang mentions in the book that although this period of Cold War history has already passed us by, its traces in fact remain to this day in our lives, in our societies and in the entire international order.

Moreover, "the story of the Cold War" presumably had both good and bad effects. Could you briefly tell everyone what positive and what negative influences the Cold War has brought us up to now?

Wang Jisi: I have really never thought about this question carefully. Because history moves in a tortuous way, what counts as positive and what counts as negative is always different. For example, the collapse of the Soviet Union was of course a bad thing for Marxism-Leninism, but in the period of history after the Soviet collapse, China still developed.

So what exactly is good and what is bad—judged from different angles, the conclusions are completely different. And what you consider good at one stage you may consider bad once another stage has passed, and this has to do with our own lives as well.

Having lived to this point, I find that many things I did or predicted in the past were wrong. For example, I did not anticipate Trump's first rise to power; the second time I had a certain premonition, but I did not fully predict it, and in particular I did not predict that after taking office he would do so many astonishing things. Many people think he is doing very well—there is no shortage of "Trump fans"—but there are also many people who do not like him. So when it comes to questions like this, in my book I also try to avoid making a very clear value judgment—I leave the value judgment to the readers, for you to think about yourselves.

It is not that I am asking you to think through something as large as this to the bottom, but rather to think about what impact such things might have on you. Whether you are reading history or reading about present realities, the lesson you should draw above all is about the impact it may have on you personally. For example, what impact will the current China-U.S. confrontation have on you? Will you go to study in the United States later? Will you make friends with Americans later? Things of that kind—this is what I hope to talk about with everyone in the book.

## 03 Are We in a "New Cold War"?

Jodie Wen: My own final question—are we really now living in a "new Cold War" era? Is the current China-U.S. relationship really like the relationship between the United States and the Soviet Union during the Cold War? Is there any way to break the impasse?

Wang Jisi: About twenty years ago, or even a dozen or so years ago, I would not have thought that a new Cold War would emerge. My view has now changed.

On the one hand, at that time there was no economic and social exchange between the United States and the Soviet Union like what exists between China and the United States today: not so many Soviet students went running off to the United States, or American students to the Soviet Union; their cultural exchanges were basically severed, and their social exchanges were severed. If we look at it from this angle, China and the United States today have a better relationship than the United States and the Soviet Union did then, so it is not a new Cold War.

On the other hand, today when the United States suffers a failure anywhere in the world or has trouble at home, we will talk about it, criticize it, expose it, and so on, while the United States also tries by every means to find fault with us and hopes to weaken China. This is the same as during the Cold War—weakening the other side by every possible means. But at that time the means of weakening the other side were far fewer than they are now.

The contradictions between China and the United States are more complex than those between the United States and the Soviet Union at that time, and one could even say more acute. America's vigilance toward us now, and our vigilance toward America, are far more complex in the areas of social exchange, academia and science and technology than they were during the Cold War.

## 04 Trust Common Sense, Be Prepared

Reader A: When doing academic study, I often have a feeling of having a surplus of knowledge but a shortage of wisdom. We learn many theories and many historical details, and we use different methods to analyze a historical event. But if we are asked to analyze the present situation, or even to predict the future, I feel at a loss as to where to begin. So I very much want to ask the professor: how can one avoid becoming a database administrator and instead truly cultivate strategic insight and the ability to judge policy?

Wang Jisi: There is only so much common sense in the world, and you have to trust common sense. And I think young people should accumulate a bit more social experience. In a recent article I said that although we constantly study foreign problems, you also need to understand more about China's social conditions, the hearts of the Chinese people and Chinese culture, and you need to understand more about other people internationally as well—human nature is in fact the same.

The earliest book on international relations I read was Kenneth Waltz's *Man, the State, and War*. That book puts human nature in first place. Are human beings fond of fighting? Possibly. Everyone likes watching martial arts films and playing games—isn't that fighting? So if you want to bring about a harmonious society or a harmonious world, that is easier said than done—this is a question of human nature.

There are many articles nowadays that I am not quite convinced by. They give you a great deal of material, more than you could imagine. But they depart from common sense, they depart from some of the most basic principles, they depart from the most basic observation of human beings. People can make very great mistakes, but human nature is shared.

What should we do in the future? Of course, we cannot change human nature. But what I often do now is communicate with foreigners—especially Americans. This may be useful, or it may not. But such communication still has a certain meaning: although it may not be able to prevent conflict, if there were no communication, things would be even more terrible.

Reader B: Trump's new policies at present seem to go against Wilsonianism and may be heading in an authoritarian direction. So what should we do at the individual level?

Wang Jisi: Make all kinds of mental preparations. Although Trump is quite unusual, he is still an American after all, so he still considers things starting from American interests. As for what path he will take, relying on my many years of understanding of the United States, I believe that some of America's characteristics will remain. Although Trump is very arrogant now and believes that he can change everything, in reality he cannot change too many things. The span of his life, too, is plainly there for all to see.

But you have to be mentally prepared for this—America will change. When I went to the United States this April, I told Americans that the America of today is not the America I once knew; they said, so it is with us, we never thought America would become like this either. So I think the profound lesson is that we must be mentally prepared.
